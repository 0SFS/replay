# Learjet 60 N722BD, 1 November 2026

Nine people died when this aircraft crashed in Palm Beach County, Florida. This document and the replay are for them. We think the best way to honour them is to understand what happened, so that it does not happen again, and to claim no more than the evidence shows.

This is an independent reading of public ADS-B data and of a public recording of air traffic control, written in the day after the accident. It is not an investigation. The NTSB investigates, and its findings will take the place of anything written here. Times are in UTC, with Eastern Standard Time (UTC−5) where it helps.

Replay: `/replay/?timeline=N722BD-2026-11-01`. Locally, run `npm run dev` in `fly` and open <https://localhost:7470/replay/?timeline=N722BD-2026-11-01>.

## The flight

N722BD took off from Austin, Texas, at 15:37, bound for North Eleuthera in the Bahamas. It cruised at 41,000 ft for 96 minutes, across the Gulf of Mexico to Florida. Over southwest Florida it descended to 35,000 ft and turned northeast. At 17:48:15 (12:48 EST), over Palm Beach County at 35,450 ft, it began a descent that ended 78 seconds later.

## What the data is, and what it is not

- **What it is:** the messages the aircraft's own transponder broadcast (ADS-B), as volunteer receivers on the ground heard them. adsb.lol's trace has 1,028 reports, from 14:17:43 on the ground at Austin to 17:49:32.76. The timeline keeps the 957 reports from 15:32, when the aircraft started to taxi.
- **What it carries:**
  - GNSS position;
  - pressure altitude;
  - GNSS altitude, a height above the WGS 84 ellipsoid, not above sea level or the ground;
  - ground speed, track and vertical rate;
  - the squawk code, the IDENT flag, the emergency status, and figures for how far the position can be trusted.
- **What it does not carry:**
  - airspeed, Mach number or heading;
  - pitch or bank;
  - autopilot modes, the selected altitude, control positions or engine data;
  - anything said on the radio.

  readsb has fields for airspeed and roll, and they are empty throughout.
- **When each report is dated:** when a receiver heard it.
- **How often reports came:** in the last minutes, every 0.4 to 3 s, with gaps. Between 17:41 and 17:47, gaps of 6 to 20 s are common. There is one gap of 12.4 s, from 17:48:00.9 to 17:48:13.3, just before the descent began. The replay interpolates across gaps, and says so when the last report is more than a few seconds old.

## What the data shows

### A growing pitch oscillation at 35,000 ft

From 17:45:50, the reported vertical rate swings between climb and descent, and the swings grow:

- In the minute from 17:47:00 to 17:48:01, reports are 0.5 to 6 s apart. The extremes of the rate are:

  | Time | Rate |
  | --- | --- |
  | 17:47:09.6 | −384 ft/min |
  | 17:47:18.5 | +512 ft/min |
  | 17:47:24.6 | −640 ft/min |
  | 17:47:35.3 | +960 ft/min |
  | 17:47:50.2 | −704 ft/min |
  | 17:48:00.9 | +1,344 ft/min |

  Successive swings the same way are 15 to 26 s apart.
- Earlier, reports are up to 12 s apart, so the start of the oscillation is less clear. There are swings of +320 and −320 ft/min at 17:46:07 and 17:46:16, and +576 ft/min at 17:46:40.
- Through all of it, pressure altitude stays between 34,950 and 35,075 ft. At this period, a swing to 1,000 ft/min and back moves the aircraft up or down only 50 to 70 ft.

**Interpretation.** Left to itself, an aircraft oscillates in pitch in two ways:

- a short-period motion of a few seconds;
- the phugoid, which at this speed takes about 100 s (π√2·V/g, with V ≈ 230 m/s).

Swings 15 to 26 s apart fit neither. That suggests something was moving the pitch controls in a loop: the autopilot, or a pilot holding the altitude, with the aircraft answering late or unevenly. Each swing larger than the one before is how such a loop looks when it is losing control rather than gaining it. Turbulence is another possibility. The data cannot tell these apart.

### The climb and the push over the top

- From 17:48:01, the aircraft climbs. The next report, 12.4 s later, is 325 ft higher.
- The climb stops at 35,450 ft at 17:48:15. The last two downward swings were 25.6 s apart, at 17:47:24.6 and 17:47:50.2. The descent began about 25 s after the second, and did not turn back.
- In the next 24 s, the vertical rate goes from zero to −20,900 ft/min. The track stays between 099° and 102°, so the aircraft is not turning, and its wings were about level.
- **Estimated load factor:** about 0.3 to 0.9 g from 17:48:15 to 17:48:35, about half a g on average. Over those 20 s the flight path went from level to 22° down, and the speed rose from about 445 to 515 kt. ("How the figures were worked out" explains these estimates.)

**Interpretation.** Suppose a stable aircraft speeds up with its controls and trim left where they were. It tends to raise its nose, unless it is near the Mach numbers where the nose drops instead (Mach tuck). So flying at under 1 g for 20 s while speeding up means something held the nose down. It could have been the elevator, the stabilizer trim, Mach tuck, or another cause. The data does not say which.

### The spiral

From 17:48:38, the descent turns into a tightening right turn. It lasts to the end.

The pattern is that of a spiral dive: the nose down, the bank growing, the turn tightening and the descent steepening. The turn never stopped, so the wings stayed banked to the right for the last 54 s, and the bank grew.

### What was said on the radio

Below, "the crew" and "Miami Center" are inferred from the words: the controller's calls begin with the call sign, and the crew's end with it. The words come from another listener's captions, and nobody here has checked them against the recording word by word.

**What the crew reported.**

- **Before the upset:**
  - at 35,000 ft, moderate turbulence;
  - an outside air temperature of −47 °C;
  - "We're managing the ice";
  - "We're direct Freeport", while the track turned toward 099°. 099° points at the Freeport VOR on Grand Bahama.
- **As it began:** precipitation, and the need to climb to FL370.
- **In the descent:**
  - descending at 8,000, then 10,000 ft/min, with a mayday;
  - an overspeed, at 20,000 ft/min;
  - that they were going to crash into the ground;
  - a descent of twenty-two thousand feet (per minute, if the alignment is right) and a 60° bank;
  - "Look outside! … we're out of time", the last words heard, at about 16,000 ft.
- **What is not heard:** no call about the flight controls is in the captions. Some words are missing from them, at 17:48:46 and from 17:49:01 to 17:49:05. The recording starts at 17:47:30, so anything said earlier is not in it.

**Interpretation.**

- **The first call already reports the oscillation.** The recording's first words, at 17:47:31, are a ride report: "three five zero, moderate turbulence, plus or minus… now seven hundred, six hundred feet". Altitude was then within 75 ft of 35,000 ft, but the reported vertical rate was swinging from −640 to +960 ft/min, so the figures read as feet per minute. The swings had begun at 17:45:50, before the recording starts.
- **The climb above 35,000 ft was not a cleared climb.** The aircraft was climbing from about 17:47:56, before the crew asked for FL370 at about 17:48:12. Miami Center cleared it to FL370 at 17:48:16–21, just after the highest point. The climb reads as the last and largest upswing of the oscillation, or as one the crew began before asking.
- **The crew called the descent about 10 s after it began.**
  - The aircraft went over the top at 17:48:15.
  - The crew read back the climb clearance at 17:48:22–24, when the aircraft was already descending at 4,000 to 5,000 ft/min.
  - They said "we're descending" at about 17:48:26.
  - A push over that took 10 s to reach 5,000 ft/min could have felt like turbulence at first.
- **The crew's "banking sixty degrees" matches the estimates.** It was said at 17:48:57–17:49:00. The bank estimated for a coordinated turn there is 57 to 59°. This supports the estimates the replay draws the model at.
- **The crew named ice.** At −47 °C, airframe ice is unusual, but ice crystals in convective cloud are not, and they can block pitot probes. A blocked probe gives the crew and the autopilot wrong airspeed or altitude. The recording does not say what ice they meant, or where it was.
- **IDENT and the crew's calls.** With this alignment, both times IDENT came on fall at the end of, or during, a crew transmission:
  - the first, between 17:48:50.4 and 17:48:50.9, as a call ended at 17:48:50.8;
  - the second, between 17:49:14.1 and 17:49:14.6, during "Look outside!".

  This could be chance. Where the IDENT button is in this cockpit has not been checked here.

### The transponder in the last minute

- **IDENT, first time.** It is clear at 17:48:48.9 and set at 17:48:50.9, about 35 s into the descent. It is still set at 17:49:10.4, and clear at 17:49:12.5.
- **IDENT, second time.** It is set again by 17:49:14.6, and stays set to the last report.
- **What IDENT is.** The IDENT flag (SPI) is set by pressing the transponder's IDENT button, and it stays on for about 18 s. Controllers usually ask for it to pick an aircraft out on radar. So someone in the cockpit pressed it at least twice during the descent.
- **No emergency code.** The squawk stays 4021 and the emergency status reads "none" throughout: 7700 was not set. A crew already talking to a controller is not expected to change its code, so this says little either way.

### The last seconds

- From 17:49:23.07, every report gives a pressure altitude of 8,750 ft. Over the same 9.7 s, GNSS altitude falls from 9,475 to 325 ft.
- At 17:49:26.57, the Navigation Integrity Category falls from 9 to 0: the transponder no longer vouches for its position. Its accuracy figure (NACp 10: better than 10 m) does not change.
- Several things could explain either of these:
  - the aircraft's systems or antennas, at that attitude and speed;
  - damage;
  - for the pressure altitude only, the receiving networks holding the last value.

  The data does not say which.
- **The last report**, at 17:49:32.76 (12:49:32.76 EST):
  - position 26.6327° N, 80.1127° W;
  - GNSS altitude 325 ft. That is about 400 ft above the ground: GNSS altitude is measured from the WGS 84 ellipsoid, which here is 91 ft above sea level, and the ground is about 17 ft above sea level;
  - descending at more than 50,000 ft/min;
  - ground speed 238 kt, track 290°.
- **To the ground, by extrapolation.** No report covers the last 400 ft. The timeline continues the track from the last report at its ground speed, turn rate (6.5°/s) and fitted descent rate (59,000 ft/min), and marks the added samples as extrapolated.
  - The path reaches the ground 0.40 s after the last report, at 17:49:33.16, about 50 m west-northwest of the last reported position.
  - At 56,000 ft/min, the average over the last 4 s, it would take 0.43 s instead.
  - The rates in the last second are not known, so the time and place are a little uncertain.

## Did the flight controls jam?

The question this work started from: did the control surfaces freeze, so that the crew could not raise the nose, and the aircraft kept pitching down until it hit the ground? Reports said the pilot told air traffic control about trouble with the controls. In the recording of the last two minutes the crew does not mention the controls. They report turbulence, ice, precipitation, an overspeed and a 60° bank. Calls before 17:47:30 are not in the recording.

### What fits

- **The descent never reversed.** Over 78 s the flight path went from level to 67° down. It held near 21° for about 15 s, then steepened again, and never turned back up.
- **Something held the nose down.** The first 20 s of the descent were flown at about half a g while the aircraft sped up. An elevator or a stabilizer stuck nose-down would do that.
- **The oscillation could be a symptom.** It could be the sign of a pitch control that was binding, or answering late, while the autopilot or a pilot tried to hold the altitude.
- **IDENT was pressed twice in the descent.** Someone in the cockpit was still working the transponder, and the crew was talking to Miami Center throughout.
- **The crew named ice.** Ice could stiffen or jam a control, though at −47 °C it is unusual.

### What needs explaining

- **Pitch was still moving both ways until 17:48:15.** The swings were growing, and the aircraft climbed 325 ft in the 12 s before the top. If the controls jammed, they jammed at about the top of that climb, or they were only partly stuck before then.
- **The crew did not say the controls had jammed.** In 50 s of calls during the descent they named the descent rate, an overspeed and the bank, but not the controls. That proves nothing: a crew in an upset says little, and some words are missing.
- **The bank kept growing.** The aircraft turned right and banked ever more steeply for 54 s. The ailerons work separately from the elevator, so with only the elevator stuck, the crew could still have rolled the wings level. That is the first step out of a spiral dive. One of these would have to be true:
  - roll control was affected too;
  - the crew could not use it, at speeds where the forces on the controls are very large;
  - the crew could not tell how the aircraft was banked.

### What the data cannot separate

Each of these would show on ADS-B as a descent that steepens into a spiral:

- an elevator or trim stuck nose-down;
- ice, on the airframe or in the pitot-static probes, which can feed wrong airspeed and altitude to the crew and the autopilot;
- a trim that ran nose-down;
- an autopilot fault;
- Mach tuck;
- a structural failure;
- an upset from turbulence;
- a crew misled about the aircraft's attitude.

Telling them apart takes evidence that ADS-B does not carry. **The hypothesis is consistent with the data, but the data does not establish it.**

## What would settle it

- **The whole air traffic control recording:** what the crew said before 17:47:30, and the words missing from the captions.
  - LiveATC.net may have recorded the frequency, if a volunteer feed covered it. Its archives are half-hour files, and the one that starts at 17:30Z covers this. LiveATC blocks scripted downloads, so it has to be downloaded in a browser. The Radio tab opens it and lines it up by its name.
  - The FAA keeps its own recordings, and the NTSB will have them.
- **The NTSB's preliminary report**, usually within a few weeks, and later the public docket in the NTSB's CAROL database.
- **The cockpit voice recorder**, and a flight data recorder if the aircraft had one.
- **The wreckage:** whether the aircraft was in one piece when it hit the ground, and where the control surfaces and the trim actuators were.
- **The weather at 35,000 ft over Palm Beach County around 17:45:**
  - SIGMETs, and reports of turbulence from other aircraft (PIREPs);
  - upper-air soundings and satellite images;
  - the wind, which would turn ground speed into airspeed.
- **FAA radar data:** an independent record of the path and altitude.
- **Maintenance records:** recent work on the flight controls or the trim, and the airworthiness directives that apply.
- **A Learjet 60 flight model:** with one, a simulation could try each failure and see which reproduces this path, the oscillation and the spiral. The Learjet 60 package has a proposal for a JSBSim model (`Learjet-60/docs/proposals/jsbsim-model.md`). The timeline format already has room for a simulated track beside the recorded one.

## About the replay

- **It opens at 17:47:30 (12:47:30 EST)**, where the radio recording starts, so that its first call, the turbulence report, plays. The oscillation in the data began earlier, at 17:45:50.
- **Attitude is estimated.** The model is drawn at the flight-path angle, and at the bank a coordinated turn would need, both worked out from the path. In this flight's last minute these can be far from the real pitch and bank.
- **The paint is not N722BD's.** The model is a Learjet 60 in another aircraft's black and silver paint (N838RC).
- **Altitude is GNSS altitude.** The aircraft is placed at its GNSS altitude, a height above the WGS 84 ellipsoid.
  - Over FOSS Earth's terrain, which is drawn at heights above sea level, the replay subtracts EGM2008's height of sea level, −91 ft here, so the aircraft is drawn about 91 ft higher than its GNSS altitude.
  - Over Google 3D Tiles, which are drawn at heights above the ellipsoid, nothing is subtracted.
- **The drawn altitude is smoothed.** GNSS altitude comes in 25 ft steps, and some reports repeat the one before. The aircraft and its path are drawn along a line fitted over ±2 s, which takes those steps out but keeps the oscillation and the dive. The readings and the chart show the reported values.
- **The dots are the reports.** Each dot on the path is a position that was reported, at the altitude reported. The map leaves out dots that would overlap. Before the last minute, 95% of them sit within about 20 ft of the smoothed path, and none more than about 50 ft. In the last 13 s of the dive they scatter up to about 225 ft either side of it, about a quarter of a second of the descent. The last 0.4 s, which was extrapolated, has no dots.
- **The radio plays along.** The Radio tab plays the recording from Wikimedia, in time with the replay at 0.25× to 2×. It lists what was said, and the Replay tab shows the words being said under the current moment. The recording is lined up to within about 2 s. The tab can move it by tenths of a second, and can open another recording, such as a LiveATC archive.
- **Values between reports are interpolated.** The readings say when the last report is more than a few seconds old.
- **The last 0.4 s is extrapolated.** From the last report to the ground, the readings say that nothing there was reported.

## How the figures were worked out

Every figure here comes from the published timeline, `public/replay/timelines/N722BD-2026-11-01.json`, or from the adsb.lol trace it is built from.

- **Vertical rate.** Reported vertical rates are as broadcast. They come in 64 ft/min steps, and are capped at ±32,640 ft/min. The fitted descent rate is the timeline's `vsFromGeomFpm`: the slope of GNSS altitude over ±2 s.
- **Flight-path angle and bank.** These are the timeline's `pitchEstDeg` and `rollEstDeg` (`src/kinematics.ts`).
- **Load factor.** The velocity comes from ground speed, track and the fitted descent rate. Its rate of change is fitted over ±2 s, and gravity is taken away. The load factor is the part of the result across the flight path, in g.
  - It is measured against the ground, so a change in wind with height shows up as error. Over this descent, that error is a few hundredths of a g.
  - GNSS altitude comes in 25 ft steps, so single values scatter by a few tenths of a g. The ranges above allow for that.
- **The ground's height.** The terrain FOSS Earth draws by default is Mapterhorn's. It gives 5.3 m (17 ft) above sea level along the last 50 m of the path, and AWS Terrain Tiles agree within a foot. Sea level there is 27.9 m (91 ft) below the WGS 84 ellipsoid, by FOSS Earth's EGM2008 grid. In GNSS altitude, the ground is 17 − 91 = −74 ft. The recipe's `extrapolate` gives both figures.
